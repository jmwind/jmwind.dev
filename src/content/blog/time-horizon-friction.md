New year, same meeting bashing. You can cancel them all you want. But they will come back if you don't see and squash one of the top meeting culprits.

Picture this: It's 1998. A group of developers are huddled around a table, shipping code. Fast forward to today - half your team is in meetings about next year's roadmap trying to fight for resourceswhile two lonely developers try to ship this week's features. What changed?

A few years ago I thought this was a problem of mega-companies. Waved it off as something I didn't have to worry about anymore. But after starting my own tiny startup it started happening again. This isn't a scale-up problem, it's an everyone problem. 

You typically have two types of people on your teams: the planners and the builders. In so many teams someone plans & specs a project, hands it over to the builders. But it's a lot longer to build something than to imagine it. So the building takes a lot longer than the planning. You repeat this cycle over and over and your company looks like this:

![before](/images/time-horizon-friction/before.png)

Here's where it gets messy: While the builders are heads-down implementing Project A, the planners have already moved on to imagining Projects B, C, and D. These future projects are exciting, right?! Who doesn't want to think about the next big thing? Part of that exploring requires meeting with the builders to get their feedback and understand what's possible. They usually can't read the code, so they book a meeting.
Now imagine you have 50% of your team as builders and 50% as planners. And every planner can have 2-3 projects in exploring, and each builder has 1 project in building. Then each planner will add 2-3x meetings to your company schedule, that isn't helping ship. Worse, now you're context switching your entire company all the time.
Let's make this concrete. Meet Sarah, a product manager (planner) working with Alex, a developer (builder):
Sarah: "Hey Alex, can we chat about this cool AI feature for Q3?"
Alex: "Sure, but I'm trying to ship the authentication update this week..."
Sarah: "It'll just take 30 minutes! Oh, and can we also discuss the mobile app redesign for Q4?"
Multiply this by five Sarahs and three Alexes, and suddenly your builders are spending more time in "quick chats" about the future than building for today.
It sounds bad, but it's very hard to diagnose. Because everyone is busy. There's a lot of important projects on the go. You're killing it.... with meetings. You want to show the CEO that you're ambitious and can get a lot done. So you pop new projects like you popped new pills in Ibiza in your 20s. Your developers are pissed because they're always in meetings about future stuff.

# The right kind of planning

I'll admit, you can't have no plans. But you can have a principles & directionality plan that can survive 6-12 months. Stop planning every detail of the future. 

# Other tops

The Real Cost of Time Horizon Friction:

Context switching kills productivity
Future planning creates false urgency
Builders get frustrated and leave
Things take longer to ship
Planning becomes a substitute for building

How do you fix this? Here's the battle plan:

Have a principles & directionality plan that can survive 6-12 months. Stop planning every detail of the future.
Put a hard limit on future planning meetings - maximum 1 hour per week with non-developers.
Recognize OODA-skilled team members (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act) more than those who hit the exact plan. The plan is the floor, not the ceiling.
Pull non-developers into today by giving them tasks for the current release. Get them invested in what's shipping now, not just what might ship later.
Reduce the number of non-today-building team members. Yes, this means fewer planners.
Don't split planning and building - have the team start and ship together. No hand-offs.
Limit explore projects to those where the team can be self-sufficient (eg, prototype, talk to customers, design)

Remember: There is zero cost in a company to start something. There is zero cost to ping someone and ask for their opinion on this newly started project. Everyone wants to be helpful. That's what makes this so dangerous - the cost only becomes visible when it's too late.
Cancel meetings all you want, but if you don't cancel projects and maniacally control your WIP (Work in Progress), those meetings will just come back. Like a bad horror movie sequel, they always come back.
The key? Reduce risk by building, not by over-planning. Because at the end of the day, shipped code beats perfect plans every time.
