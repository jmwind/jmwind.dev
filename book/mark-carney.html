Chapter 12

I interviewed Mark Carney for a job

It was 2021. We were looking for a board member or executive who could help us think about entrepreneurship in the context of the global economy.

I was asked to interview a guy named Mark. I Googled him. He had a decent CV.

After brief introductions, he jumped straight in: "I see on Strava that you're a runner and you live in..." It threw me off a bit. He had clearly done his homework. Borderline stalking. We talked about running for a while. He was good at small talk. He casually mentioned that he runs marathons now and then.

Mental note: he does his homework. Also, is he distracting me so I don't get to my questions? I started worrying he'd try to schmooze me instead of getting into anything substantive.

Argue the other side

In my defence, I'd done some homework too. I hadn't stalked him on social media, but I'd read several articles he'd written. He leaned strongly globalist and believed most of our challenges don't respect borders and will only be solved collaboratively.

I wanted to see if he could argue against his own position. So I asked: when should countries invest in self-reliance? For context, I explained that in software we've learned purely centralized systems fail in obvious ways. But overly distributed systems fail too, when small issues propagate across too many dependencies.

Do societies behave the same way? Are we sometimes too decentralized? When should a country accept less efficiency and invest in more centralization or self-reliance?

He smiled. I couldn't tell whether he thought the question was childish or whether I'd annoyed him. Then he broke the silence: "This is a great parallel. Give me a second to think about it."

We ended up having a great conversation. That said, it took him a lot of words to make his point. Professional talker.

He liked the exercise. I could tell he'd spent so long defending global collaboration that he hadn't fully prepared for this angle. I didn't know it at the time, but he was in the middle of writing Value(s), which is essentially an ode to global cooperation.

We went over time. It didn't faze him. He cared about finishing the discussion. By then he was improvising, and it felt natural and fun. A genuinely thoughtful discussion. I learned a lot.

His moment

In the end, he didn't join us. But we all wanted him to.

When I see him in his current gig, a small part of me laughs that I might have helped warm him up.

The more I think about that conversation, the clearer it becomes that he probably didn't want the Prime Minister job. Not in the way people want promotions or titles. Some people spend a lifetime preparing for problems they hope never arrive. When the moment shows up anyway, they step in. Not because it's appealing, but because it's necessary.

This just happened to be his moment.
